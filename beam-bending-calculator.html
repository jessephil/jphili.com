Beam Bending Calculator

Multi-support beams with simple or fixed supports, point loads, point moments, and distributed loads — solved with the direct stiffness (matrix) method, which handles statically indeterminate configurations directly.


Beam Properties

Cross-Section (Moment of Inertia)

For a beam or shaft whose cross-section changes along its length, add sections to step the moment of inertia. Each section runs from the end of the previous one; the last always extends to the end of the beam. Modulus of elasticity E is assumed uniform (same material throughout).

Supports

Simple = pin/roller (restrains deflection only). Fixed = restrains deflection and rotation. Bearing = elastic radial support with a finite stiffness, useful for shafts (restrains deflection only, like Simple, but elastically rather than rigidly). Two or more simple/bearing supports, or a single fixed support, are required for stability.

Loads

Point loads and distributed loads act downward when positive. Moments act counterclockwise when positive.

Method — Direct Stiffness (Matrix) Analysis

The beam is discretized into Euler–Bernoulli elements between every support and load location. This treats determinate and indeterminate (multi-support, mixed simple/fixed) beams identically, without special-case logic per configuration.

k = (EI/L_e³)·[12, 6L_e, −12, 6L_e; 6L_e, 4L_e², −6L_e, 2L_e²; ...] EI·d²y/dx² = M(x),   dM/dx = V(x)

Distributed loads are converted to equivalent fixed-end nodal forces before assembly. Within each span, deflection is recovered as a closed-form quartic satisfying the solved end deflections/rotations plus any distributed load on that span — giving exact V(x), M(x), and y(x) between nodes rather than linear interpolation.

EI = flexural rigidity L_e = element (span) length M = bending moment, sagging positive V = shear force y = deflection, downward positive

Bearing supports. A bearing is modeled as a linear elastic radial spring (reaction = k·δ) added directly into the stiffness matrix, rather than as a rigid constraint — so the shaft is free to deflect at that location, and the stiffer the bearing, the closer the result converges to a rigid simple support. Like a simple support, a bearing restrains radial deflection only, not rotation. Radial stiffness is assumed the same in every direction (isotropic), so it applies unchanged to both bending planes below.

Angled point loads (biaxial bending). A point load applied at an angle is resolved into a Y-component (P·cosθ) and a Z-component (P·sinθ), each solved as a fully independent beam problem sharing the same supports and cross-section. This is exact — not an approximation — for a shaft whose cross-section has equal moment of inertia in every direction (i.e. round or otherwise doubly-symmetric), since bending in the two planes is then completely uncoupled. Distributed loads and applied moments are not angled and act only in the Y-plane. Shear, moment, and deflection diagrams show the Y-component, Z-component, and resultant magnitude (√(Y²+Z²)) together; reactions and fixed-end moments on the beam diagram show the resultant with a (Y, Z) breakdown. Angle is measured counterclockwise from straight down, viewed looking along +X from the beam's start — see the End View diagram. Note: for a single load, the resultant magnitude is mathematically identical regardless of angle — only its Y/Z split changes.

Sign convention: downward loads positive, sagging moment positive, deflection computed positive downward but plotted as negative (dipping below the axis) so the deflection diagram visually matches the direction of a downward load. Moment of inertia may vary in piecewise-constant sections along the beam; each section boundary is inserted as an analysis node so displacement, rotation, shear, and moment stay exactly continuous across the step. Modulus of elasticity E is assumed uniform (single material). Method verified against closed-form and independent virtual-work solutions for simply-supported, cantilever, propped-cantilever, continuous-beam, and stepped-section cases. References: Euler–Bernoulli beam theory; McGuire, Gallagher & Ziemian, Matrix Structural Analysis; Hibbeler, Mechanics of Materials.
