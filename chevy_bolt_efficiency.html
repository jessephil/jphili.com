How this is calculated

The numbers come from a physics model of steady-speed driving on flat ground, not from logged Bolt data. It adds up air drag, tire rolling resistance, drivetrain and battery losses, accessories, and cabin heating or cooling.

  • Cold air is denser, so drag rises. Tires also roll harder when cold.
  • Below about 50°F the battery and motor lose some efficiency, and usable battery capacity shrinks a little.
  • Heating draw grows as it gets colder and with speed, and is capped at a typical resistive heater limit. Cooling starts above about 75°F.

To make it match your car: drive a steady highway stretch around 70°F and 70 mph, then change “Adjust to my car” until that cell matches what your car showed. Everything else scales with it.

Not modeled: headwind, hills, rain or snow on the road, winter tires, and preconditioning while plugged in. Treat the cold-weather numbers as a planning estimate and keep a buffer.
